There is a question I ask early in almost every client conversation: what does your reporting actually tell you?

The answers are usually some variation of the same thing. We have dashboards. We have monthly reports. We have spreadsheets that go to the senior leadership team every week. And when I ask what decisions those reports have driven recently, the room goes quiet.

This is the difference between data reporting and data insight. And it is one of the most common, most expensive, and least talked about problems in organisations that think they are doing well with data.

## Reporting tells you what happened. Insight tells you what to do about it.

A report is a summary of data. It might tell you that student satisfaction scores dropped by four points last quarter, that staff turnover increased by twelve percent, or that your top product line is underperforming against target. These are facts. They are useful. But they are not insight.

Insight is what happens when you ask the next question. Why did satisfaction drop? Was it concentrated in a particular cohort, a particular service, a particular period? Is the turnover increase happening across the organisation or in one team? What is driving the underperformance and what would need to change to fix it?

Most organisations stop at the report. The insight is left as an exercise for whoever is sitting in the meeting, working from memory and instinct rather than data.

## A real example

An organisation I worked with had a well-established reporting function. Every month, a pack of around thirty slides went to the senior leadership team covering performance across every major area of the business. It took two analysts most of the week to produce.

When I sat in on one of those meetings, something struck me. The pack was comprehensive. It covered everything. And the leadership team spent most of the meeting debating which numbers were correct, not what to do about them.

The problem was not the data. The data was largely accurate. The problem was that the reports had been designed to cover everything rather than to answer anything. There was no clear narrative. No signal separated from noise. No indication of which metrics were moving in the wrong direction and why.

We spent three months rebuilding the reporting framework around a much smaller set of questions the leadership team actually needed to answer each month. The pack went from thirty slides to eight. The analyst time halved. And for the first time, the monthly meeting was spent making decisions rather than questioning the data.

## Why this happens

Organisations default to reporting for a few reasons. Reporting feels safe; it is hard to argue with a number. Insight requires interpretation, and interpretation requires someone to make a call that might be wrong. There is also a culture in many organisations where producing data is seen as the job done. The analysis and the action that should follow are treated as someone else's problem.

The other reason is tool-led thinking. A new BI platform arrives, everyone gets access, and dashboards get built to replicate the spreadsheets that came before. The technology changes but the underlying question, what are we actually trying to understand, never gets asked.

## What good looks like

The organisations that do this well share a few characteristics. Their reporting is built around decisions, not metrics. They have a small number of genuinely important questions that data is expected to answer each period. Their analysts spend more time on interpretation than on production. And their leadership teams treat data as the start of a conversation, not the end of one.

Getting from reporting to insight is not primarily a technology problem. It is a design problem. It starts with being clear about what decisions your data needs to support, and working backwards from there.

If your reporting is comprehensive but your meetings still feel like guesswork, that is usually the sign that something needs to change.
