There is a question worth asking of any data or analytics team: what percentage of your time is spent building and maintaining reports versus actually analysing data?

In most organisations the answer is uncomfortable. Analysts spend the majority of their time producing, formatting, and distributing reports that look largely the same every month. The actual analysis, the work that generates insight and drives decisions, gets squeezed into whatever time is left over.

This is a waste of some of the most valuable capability an organisation has. Here are five things your analysts should be spending their time on instead.

## Answering questions that nobody has thought to ask yet

The best analysts do not just respond to requests. They proactively look for patterns, anomalies, and trends that the organisation does not yet know it should be paying attention to. A drop in a metric that nobody is currently tracking. A correlation between two datasets that nobody has connected before. An emerging trend that will become a problem in six months if nothing changes now.

This kind of proactive analysis is only possible when analysts are not buried under a backlog of report requests.

## Improving the quality of the questions being asked

Most organisations ask their analysts the wrong questions. Not because the questions are unimportant, but because they are often too narrow or too focused on what has already happened rather than what should happen next.

A good analyst challenges the question as much as they answer it. If a director asks why sales dropped last quarter, the analyst should be exploring whether that is even the right thing to be measuring, whether the comparison period is meaningful, and what the underlying drivers might be. That kind of critical thinking only happens when there is time and space for it.

## Building the capability of the people around them

One of the most valuable things an analyst can do is make the rest of the organisation more data literate. That means helping colleagues understand how to interpret data correctly, showing teams how to use self-serve tools effectively, and building the kind of shared understanding of metrics and definitions that makes reporting trustworthy.

A public sector organisation we worked with had a small but talented analytics team that spent almost all of its time responding to ad hoc data requests from across the business. When we helped them shift to a model where they spent part of their time training teams to answer their own basic questions, the volume of ad hoc requests dropped significantly. The analysts got their time back. And the wider organisation became measurably more confident in using data day to day.

## Documenting and improving data processes

Most analytics teams operate with a significant amount of tribal knowledge. The logic behind a particular calculation lives in one person's head. The reason a certain data source is excluded from a report is understood by the analyst who built it three years ago and nobody else.

When analysts have time to document what they do and why, the whole organisation benefits. Reports become reproducible. Onboarding new team members becomes easier. And the risk of something breaking and nobody knowing how to fix it reduces dramatically.

## Evaluating whether the work they are doing is actually useful

This one is uncomfortable but important. Not everything an analyst produces gets used. Some reports are opened once and never looked at again. Some dashboards were built for a stakeholder who has since left the organisation. Some analyses get produced monthly out of habit rather than need.

Analysts should be regularly reviewing what they produce and asking honestly whether it is still serving a purpose. Stopping work that is no longer useful is not a failure. It is good practice, and it frees up time for work that actually matters.

## The common thread

All five of these things require time that most analysts do not currently have because that time is consumed by report production. The solution is not to work harder. It is to design the reporting function around decisions rather than around outputs, automate or streamline the routine work where possible, and protect analyst time for the work that actually requires analytical thinking.
