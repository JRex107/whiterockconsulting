There is a particular kind of paralysis that sets in when an organisation realises its data cannot be trusted. Reports get questioned. Decisions get delayed. And somewhere along the line, someone says the words that every data team dreads: we need to fix the data before we can do anything with it.

The problem is that fixing the data feels like an enormous, undefined task. And so organisations do one of two things. They either embark on a massive data cleansing project that runs over budget and never quite finishes, or they decide the problem is too hard and quietly carry on as before.

Neither works. Here is a more practical approach.

## Accept that perfect data is not the goal

The first thing to establish is that you are not trying to achieve perfect data quality. You are trying to achieve data that is good enough to support the decisions you need to make. That is a much smaller and more achievable target.

Not all data needs to be clean. It needs to be clean enough for its purpose. A report on customer retention does not require every single field in your CRM to be accurate. It requires the fields that feed into that report to be accurate. Starting there is far more practical than trying to fix everything at once.

## Find out where the problems actually are

Before you can fix anything, you need to understand what you are dealing with. This means doing a proper data audit, not a high-level review, but a systematic look at the data that matters most to your organisation.

What are the key datasets your decisions depend on? What is the quality of those datasets today? Where are the errors, gaps, and inconsistencies coming from? In our experience, the answers to those questions almost always point to a small number of root causes. A system that does not validate inputs. A process that relies on manual data entry without any checks. A handoff between two teams where something gets lost in translation.

## Fix the source, not just the symptom

One of the most common mistakes in data quality work is cleaning data without fixing the process that created the bad data in the first place. You spend weeks correcting records, and six months later the same problems have crept back in.

A public sector organisation we worked with had a recurring issue with incomplete address data across several of their key systems. Every quarter, the data team would run a cleansing exercise to fix the gaps. And every quarter, the gaps came back.

When we looked at the root cause, the problem was straightforward. The system used to capture addresses did not require a postcode to be entered. Advisors were skipping the field under time pressure, and nobody had ever made it mandatory. One configuration change fixed a problem that had been causing quarterly cleansing exercises for three years.

## Prioritise by impact, not by volume

When data quality issues are widespread, it is tempting to prioritise by volume. Fix the fields with the most errors first. But the more useful question is: which data quality issues are causing the most harm to decision making right now?

Prioritising by impact means you get tangible results faster. Leadership teams start to trust the reporting sooner. And the momentum from early wins makes it easier to sustain the longer-term work.

## Build in ongoing monitoring

Data quality is not a project with an end date. It is an ongoing discipline. Once you have fixed the most critical issues and addressed the root causes, you need a way to monitor quality over time so problems get caught early rather than discovered in a board meeting.

This does not need to be complicated. A simple set of data quality checks run on a regular schedule, with someone responsible for reviewing and acting on the results, is enough to catch most issues before they become serious.

## The bottom line

Poor data quality is fixable in almost every organisation. The key is not to treat it as an all-or-nothing problem. Start with the data that matters most, fix the processes that are creating the problems, and build in monitoring to keep things on track.
