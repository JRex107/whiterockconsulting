There is a lot of noise at the moment about data strategy. Every consultancy, every software vendor, every conference keynote seems to be telling organisations they need one urgently. And while that is often true, it is not always true. Rushing into a strategy before you are ready is one of the fastest ways to waste time, money, and goodwill.

So before asking what your data strategy should say, it is worth asking a more honest question: are you actually ready for one?

Here are five signs that the answer is yes.

## You keep making decisions without confidence in the numbers

If your senior team regularly questions the figures in reports, if different departments produce different versions of the same metric, or if people routinely go off-piste and build their own spreadsheets because they do not trust the official data, that is a signal. It does not mean your data is beyond saving. It means the gap between the data you have and the decisions you need to make has become wide enough to cause real problems. A data strategy will not fix that overnight, but it is the right starting point.

## You have invested in tools but not seen the return

A lot of organisations have spent significant money on data platforms, BI tools, or CRM systems that are being used at a fraction of their potential. The dashboards exist but nobody looks at them. The reports get produced but nobody acts on them. If this sounds familiar, the issue is rarely the technology; it is the absence of a clear strategy for what the technology is supposed to achieve. Before buying the next tool, it is worth working out what you are actually trying to do with your data.

## You are growing and your data infrastructure has not kept up

Growth creates data complexity. New teams, new processes, new systems. If nobody is actively managing how data flows across all of them, the gaps and inconsistencies compound quickly. Organisations that are scaling tend to hit a point where the informal approach to data that worked when they were smaller simply stops working. If you are at that point, a data strategy is not a luxury; it is a practical necessity.

## Someone senior has started asking harder questions

When a CEO, director, or board member starts asking why certain things cannot be measured, why reports take so long to produce, or why the organisation cannot answer a seemingly simple question about its own performance, that is a window of opportunity. Senior sponsorship is one of the most important ingredients for a successful data strategy. If someone at the top is already asking the right questions, the conditions for change are more favourable than they might usually be.

## You have good people who are spending their time on the wrong things

If your analysts are spending most of their time cleaning data, chasing figures, and producing the same reports in slightly different formats for different stakeholders, rather than doing actual analysis, something is wrong with the underlying setup. That is not an analyst problem; it is a data infrastructure problem. A clear strategy, with proper governance and a single source of truth, is what frees those people up to do the work they were actually hired to do.

## What to do if you recognise these signs

Recognising them is the first step. The second is resisting the temptation to jump straight to solutions. The most effective data strategies start with an honest audit of where you are: your data landscape, your capability, your governance gaps. Only then should any decisions be made about where you are going.

If several of these signs resonate, it is probably worth having a conversation about what a realistic starting point looks like for your organisation. There is no universal template, but there is almost always a sensible place to begin.
