Ask ten people in your organisation what your customer retention rate is, and you might get ten different answers. Not because anyone is wrong, exactly. But because one person is pulling from the CRM, another from a spreadsheet their team has maintained for three years, and a third from a report that someone built in a hurry before a board meeting and nobody has updated since.

This is what it looks like when an organisation does not have a single source of truth. And it is far more common than most leadership teams realise.

## So what actually is a single source of truth?

A single source of truth is not a piece of software or a dashboard. It is a principle: the idea that for any given metric or dataset, there is one authoritative version that everyone in the organisation uses and trusts.

In practice, it means that when a finance director and a sales director sit down to discuss revenue performance, they are looking at the same number, from the same place, calculated the same way. Not two numbers that are close but not identical, with twenty minutes spent at the start of every meeting trying to work out which one is right.

## Why does it matter?

The obvious answer is efficiency. Organisations without a single source of truth spend enormous amounts of time reconciling data rather than acting on it. Analysts chase discrepancies. Managers build parallel spreadsheets. Leaders lose confidence in the reporting they are supposed to be using to run the organisation.

But the less obvious answer is trust. When people cannot rely on the data, they stop using it. They revert to gut instinct, personal experience, or whoever shouted loudest in the last meeting. A single source of truth is not just about having clean data; it is about creating the conditions where data-driven decisions are actually possible.

## What gets in the way

The most common barrier is not technical. It is organisational. Different teams have built their own data processes over time, often for entirely legitimate reasons. The marketing team needed a metric that the central reporting team could not produce quickly enough, so they built it themselves. The operations team inherited a legacy system that does not talk to anything else. The finance team has always done things their own way and sees no reason to change.

None of these decisions were wrong at the time. But over time they add up to a fragmented data landscape where the same concept means different things in different parts of the business.

The second barrier is governance. Even when organisations agree in principle that a single source of truth is a good idea, they often struggle to agree on definitions. What counts as an active customer? When does a lead become an opportunity? These questions sound simple but they involve real decisions about how the business works, and getting alignment on them takes more than a technical fix.

## How do you get there?

The starting point is usually an audit. Before you can consolidate your data, you need to understand what you have, where it lives, and where the inconsistencies are coming from. That is often more revealing than organisations expect.

From there, the work involves agreeing on definitions, identifying which systems should be the authoritative source for which data, and building the processes and governance to keep things consistent over time. Technology plays a role, but it is a supporting role. The harder work is the organisational alignment that has to happen first.

It is also worth being realistic about scope. You do not need to solve everything at once. Picking the two or three metrics that cause the most friction and getting those right first is a far more effective approach than trying to boil the ocean.

## The bottom line

A single source of truth is not a luxury for large enterprises. It is a practical requirement for any organisation that wants to make confident decisions based on its data. If your meetings regularly start with a debate about which numbers to use, that is a sign you do not have one yet. And that is worth fixing.
