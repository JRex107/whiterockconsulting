Every few years, an organisation decides it is finally going to get serious about data. A working group forms. A consultant is brought in. A strategy document is produced. And then, about six months later, very little has actually changed.

This is not a rare story. It is the default outcome. Most data strategies do not fail at the execution stage, they fail long before that, in the decisions made (and not made) at the very beginning.

Having worked across data and insight functions in multiple sectors, I have seen the same patterns repeat across very different organisations. Here are the ones that cause the most damage.

## The strategy is written for the wrong audience

A data strategy that impresses a board but confuses the people who have to deliver it is not a strategy, it is a document. 

The best strategies I have seen are written with the delivery team in mind first. They are specific about what needs to change, who is responsible, and what done looks like. Vague ambitions like "become a data-driven organisation" are not goals. They are aspirations dressed up as goals, and they are almost impossible to make progress against.

## Nobody has mapped what data actually exists

Organisations routinely design ambitious data strategies without first understanding what data they already have, where it lives, and whether it can be trusted. This creates a fundamental problem: you cannot build on a foundation you have not inspected. 

Before any strategy work begins, someone needs to do the unglamorous work of auditing your data landscape. What systems hold what data? Where are the gaps? Where is the quality poor? The answers to those questions should shape everything that comes after.

## The people who use data every day are not involved

Data strategies are often designed from the top down, by senior leaders and external consultants who may have limited visibility of how data actually flows through the organisation day to day. The people who spot data quality issues, work around broken processes, and know which reports are actually used versus which ones are produced and ignored, those people are rarely in the room when the strategy is written. Getting them involved early is not just good practice. It is how you avoid building something that looks coherent on paper but falls apart on contact with reality.

## Technology is treated as the solution rather than the enabler

A new data platform will not fix a culture that does not trust its data. A new dashboard tool will not make your reporting meaningful if nobody agrees on what the key metrics should be. Technology is genuinely useful, but only once the harder questions about governance, ownership, and purpose have been worked through. Too many strategies skip straight to the tool selection and wonder why adoption is low eighteen months later.

## There is no plan for building capability

Even a well-designed data strategy will stall if the organisation does not have the skills to deliver it. This does not mean hiring a team of data scientists. It means making sure the right people have access to the right training, that there are clear owners for data quality in each area, and that leadership understands enough about data to make good decisions about it. Capability is not a nice-to-have, it is the thing that determines whether the strategy outlasts the people who wrote it.

## So what does a good start look like?

It starts with honesty about where you actually are, not where you would like to be. It involves the people closest to the data, not just the people with the most authority over it. It treats technology as one piece of a larger puzzle, and it builds in a realistic plan for growing the skills and ownership needed to make the strategy stick.

None of that is complicated. But it does require slowing down at the start, which is exactly what most organisations are reluctant to do.
