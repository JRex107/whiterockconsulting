There is a particular kind of disappointment that comes from spending weeks building a dashboard, presenting it to the senior leadership team, watching them nod appreciatively, and then checking the usage data three months later to find that nobody has opened it since.

It happens more often than most data teams would like to admit. And the reason is almost never the technology.

Here is what actually makes the difference.

## Start with the decisions, not the data

The most common mistake in dashboard development is starting with the data you have rather than the decisions the leadership team needs to make. The result is a dashboard that displays everything and answers nothing.

Before you open Power BI, sit down with your key stakeholders and ask one question: what decisions do you need to make each month that you currently cannot make with confidence? The answers to that question should become the foundation of your dashboard. If a metric does not connect to a decision, it probably does not belong on the screen.

## Limit the number of metrics

There is a temptation to include everything, partly because it feels safer and partly because it demonstrates how much data you have access to. Resist it. A senior leadership team looking at thirty metrics on a single page will absorb none of them.

A good rule of thumb is no more than eight to ten key metrics on a single view. If you need to show more, use navigation or drill-through pages to separate strategic overview from operational detail. The top level should tell the story. The detail should be available for those who want to go deeper.

## Design for the question, not the data source

Every chart, table, and card on your dashboard should be there because it answers a specific question. Before adding any visual, ask: what question does this answer, and who is asking it? If you cannot answer that clearly, the visual probably should not be there.

This also means choosing the right visual for the question. A bar chart is better than a table for comparing performance across teams. A trend line is better than a single number for understanding whether performance is improving or declining. The choice of visual is not aesthetic; it is functional.

## Get the language right

One of the most overlooked causes of dashboard abandonment is terminology. If the metrics on screen are named after system fields, database tables, or internal jargon that the leadership team does not use day to day, they will not trust what they are looking at.

Use the language your audience uses. If the leadership team calls it headcount, do not label it FTE. If they talk about customer complaints, do not call it service incidents. Small things, but they matter enormously for adoption.

## Build it with them, not for them

The dashboards that get used are almost always the ones that were built collaboratively. That means sharing work in progress early, getting feedback before the final version is complete, and being willing to change things based on what you hear.

It also means building in a feedback loop after launch. The first version of any dashboard is rarely the best version. The teams that treat launch as the beginning of a process rather than the end of one end up with dashboards that genuinely embed into how the organisation works.

## Test it before you present it

Before you show a dashboard to the leadership team for the first time, test it with someone who will use it regularly but is not in the room when decisions get made. Watch how they interact with it. Note where they get confused, what they cannot find, and what questions they ask that the dashboard does not answer. Then fix those things before the main presentation.

First impressions matter. A dashboard that confuses people in its first outing rarely gets a second chance.

## The bottom line

Building a dashboard your senior leadership team will actually use is less about technical skill and more about understanding your audience. Start with their decisions, use their language, limit what you show, and build it with them rather than for them. The technology is the easy part.
